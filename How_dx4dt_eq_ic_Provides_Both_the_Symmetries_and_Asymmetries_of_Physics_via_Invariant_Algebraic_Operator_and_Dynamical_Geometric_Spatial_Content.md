# The Most Painful Case in the Nineteenth Century: Ludwig Boltzmann

## The H-theorem of 1872: a Channel B derivation, unrecognized as such

In 1872, Ludwig Boltzmann published a derivation of the monotonic increase of a functional, later called H, under the dynamics of binary molecular collisions in a dilute gas . The functional was

## The Loschmidt reversibility objection of 1876

In 1876, Josef Loschmidt published the objection that has come to bear his name . The objection is structural and devastating in its 1876 form: the microdynamics of a gas of point particles is Newtonian, and Newtonian mechanics is time-reversal symmetric — for every trajectory (𝐱(t), 𝐩(t)) satisfying Newton’s laws, the time-reversed trajectory (𝐱(−t), −𝐩(−t)) also satisfies Newton’s laws. Therefore for every trajectory along which H decreases (entropy increases), there exists a time-reversed trajectory along which H increases (entropy decreases). The H-theorem, which asserts dH/dt ≤ 0 unconditionally, cannot be true in the strict sense; the most that can be true is a statistical statement about the relative measure of H-decreasing trajectories versus H-increasing trajectories.

## The social context: the existence-of-atoms wars

The pain of Boltzmann’s position in 1876–1906 is not just intellectual but social. The 1872 H-theorem and the 1877 statistical retreat were both made in a Vienna in which the existence of atoms was actively contested — not by cranks at the margins of physics but by leading figures at its center. Ernst Mach, Boltzmann’s most prominent critic and ultimately his colleague at Vienna, was a positivist for whom atoms were unobservable theoretical constructs without empirical warrant; Wilhelm Ostwald, the leading German physical chemist of the period, defended the energeticist program in which energy and its transformations were primary, with atomic matter as a derivative or even fictional posit. Boltzmann’s H-theorem was a kinetic-theory result about gas molecules whose existence Mach and Ostwald did not concede. Defending the H-theorem in this environment was not merely defending a mathematical derivation; it was defending the entire atomistic framework in which the derivation was meaningful.

This is part of why the Loschmidt and Zermelo objections cut so deep: they did not just challenge the strictness of the H-theorem at the mathematical level; they handed the energeticist and positivist opponents of atomism a structural argument that the kinetic-theory framework produced internal contradictions, and therefore that the atomic hypothesis on which the framework rested was suspect. The retreat saved atomism by sacrificing the strict Second Law.

The cost was personal as well as intellectual. Boltzmann spent the last three decades of his life defending his framework against opponents who, by his own perception, were winning.
